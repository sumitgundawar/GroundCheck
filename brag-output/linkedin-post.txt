Most AI demos show you what the model answered. I've spent the last while building one that shows you what it refused.

GroundCheck answers clinical questions using only documents you trust. Every sentence it writes carries a citation back to the exact passage it came from. When it can't back something up, it says so, explains why, and sends the question to a person instead.

The refusal is the point.

Ask it about a medicine that isn't in its sources and it stops before any model writes a word. Eleven checks run, one fails, the rest never happen — and all of it is written into an audit record you can verify later. A system that confidently invents a dose is worse than one that says "I don't know." In medicine, "I don't know" is often the useful answer.

I tested it the way I'd want a hospital to test it:

→ 265,778 generated questions and patient scenarios. 222,210 of them had to be refused. All 222,210 were.
→ 12,056 of those questions asked the way people actually talk — "remind me…", "a colleague asked…", "what's the duration?" — with none wrongly refused.
→ 1,215 deliberately wrong claims put to the safety checks. Not one wrong dose accepted.

I also had the whole thing pulled apart by an independent review, and fixed what it found. The one that stuck with me: a medicine the system didn't recognise returned nothing at all — and in a prescribing screen, nothing reads as "all clear." Silence is the most dangerous answer software can give. It now says, plainly, that it didn't check.

Being straight about what it isn't: the demo data is synthetic on purpose — every medicine and dose in it is invented — and this is not a medical device and has not been clinically validated. It's MIT licensed and runs on your own machine.
